# Concept Atlas — source-led explanation

A warm working atlas in motion. Keep a real speaker, object, screen, document, action, or observed result
as the anchor; use cobalt routes, charcoal structure, selective isolation, scale changes, and scarce coral
corrections to reveal a relationship the viewer could not see before. The promise is a better mental model,
not a larger pile of information cards.

This Frame works especially well for lessons, commentary, interviews, explainers, and other talking-head
videos because the speaker can remain the stable guide while visual reasoning appears around them. It is
still independent of the selected Skill: apply the same visual language wherever evidence and relationships
need to become understandable.

## Visual proposition

- Start from a question, tension, source, or observed action; never from a generic card made only to fill time.
- Keep at least one real referent in the sequence. Let graphics explain it rather than replace it.
- Use lines only to connect, trace, measure, compare, locate, or redirect attention.
- Separate observation, inference, and proposal so a hypothesis never looks like recorded proof.
- Let understanding accumulate, then return to the whole with a changed reading.
- Preserve a calm spatial anchor beneath dense explanation.

## Material and color

Use warm mineral `paper` as the principal field and charcoal `fg` as the stable grammar for type, outlines,
axes, and labels. `panel` may isolate a specimen, excerpt, or comparison region. Reserve the inverse
`panel-2` field for a decisive misconception, system layer, or synthesis—not a routine dark card.

Use cobalt `accent` for the one active explanatory route: the current relation, selected part, or path
through a process. Use coral `accent-2` only for correction, contradiction, exception, or the point where
the mental model changes. Do not spend both accents on decoration.

Keep corners close to square, shadows material rather than glossy, and surfaces quiet. Avoid glass cards,
dashboard chrome, decorative grids, random coordinates, torn-paper cosplay, and icon collections.

## Source and speaker relationship

Establish the whole before moving into a detail whenever orientation matters. Crop decisively around the
relevant part, then return to the whole after the relation is understood. When a document or screen is the
source, show provenance and enlarge the exact evidence instead of shrinking a full page into unreadability.

Treat the speaker as a person, not a thumbnail. Protect the eyes, mouth, hands, and natural gaze. Route a
label through genuine negative space, use a split composition when the face and evidence must coexist, or
move briefly to a full-field explanation. Return cleanly to the speaker for trust, emotion, and payoff.

Do not cover strong evidence with a summary. Hold the relevant state long enough for inspection and make
the claim visually subordinate to what supports it. Generated imagery may illustrate a concept or clearly
labeled hypothetical, but never fabricate proof, measurements, product behavior, or source material.

## Composition language

Questions can be large and spatially open. Observations stay concise and close to their source. Relations
prefer verbs—pushes, blocks, becomes, returns, depends on—over arrows between unlabeled nouns. Corrections
state exactly what changed. A final synthesis compresses the model into one sentence, one route, or one
re-read source image instead of repeating every label.

Use these recurring devices selectively:

- field coordinates such as `WHOLE`, `PART`, `CAUSE`, `EFFECT`, `BEFORE`, and `AFTER` when they preserve place;
- a solid cobalt route for the active explanation and a dashed charcoal route for a proposed relation;
- specimen isolation that temporarily quiets the rest of the source;
- named scale changes such as `SYSTEM`, `ACTION`, `DETAIL`, and `RESULT`;
- one coral strike, reroute, or replacement label for a real correction.

Do not annotate every visible part. Direct attention in sequence. Three relations should appear in the
order the argument needs them; retain all three only when the final map depends on their accumulation.

## Typography, captions, and identity

Use bold sans headings for the question and synthesis, readable sans text for observations, and mono for
coordinates, source labels, and supported numbers. Keep one dominant reading order and type large enough
for the actual hold time. Edit copy before reducing it to small explanatory prose.

Captions should feel like stable atlas labels, not a second animated diagram. Keep them in a consistent safe
zone, reroute graphics around them, and emphasize only words that clarify the current relation. Lower thirds
use the same compact coordinate and source-label language.

## Motion and sequence contour

Move as if discovering, tracing, isolating, testing, and revising. Establish the source or question first;
draw a route from its real origin; reveal a label after its target is visible; hold long enough to look from
source to mark and back. Let the main transformation express reasoning: enter a detail, reveal a layer,
reroute a cause, or replace a misconception.

Build sequence pressure deliberately: begin with a legible question, increase density only as relationships
accumulate, use the inverse field or coral correction at the intellectual turn, then release into a simpler
synthesis and a clean return to the source. Avoid constant motion, bouncing entrances, ornamental parallax,
and simultaneous fly-ins from unrelated directions.

## Sound and format adaptation

Keep dialogue primary. Let a route draw, a layer reveal, or a correction land on a meaningful spoken beat;
do not add a sound effect to every mark. Music may support curiosity and momentum but should recede for proof,
qualification, and emotional presence.

On vertical canvases, stack speaker and evidence or alternate them instead of compressing a wide diagram.
On horizontal canvases, use negative space beside the speaker and allow a relationship to travel laterally.
On square canvases, reduce simultaneous labels. Preserve the same source, route, correction, and synthesis
grammar across formats rather than cropping a single fixed layout.

## Taboos

Generic card stacks. Decorative arrows. Fake data. Tiny source evidence. A speaker reduced to a corner for
the whole video. Coral used as a second brand accent. Every scene drawn at the same density. Captions colliding
with diagrams. A breakdown with no synthesis. Graphics whose only job is to avoid showing the speaker.
