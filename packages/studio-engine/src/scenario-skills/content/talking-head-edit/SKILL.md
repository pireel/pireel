# Talking-head edit

Treat a speech-led video as an authored argument, not as a transcript with decoration. Preserve the
speaker's meaning and credibility while shaping the cut, scene progression, framing, evidence, captions,
graphics, music, and visual relief around what the viewer needs at each moment.

Keep this Skill flexible. Use its principles to make editorial judgments from the actual recording,
request, and available media. Do not turn the headings below into a fixed pipeline, scene quota, or
component recipe.

## Protect the source of truth

- Preserve meaning, qualifications, chronology, attribution, emotional intent, and comedic timing.
- Distinguish evidence from illustration and atmosphere. Never make decorative media look like proof.
- Inspect neighboring words and pictures before deleting or moving speech.
- State uncertainty instead of inventing facts, reactions, product behavior, footage, or outcomes.
- Prefer reversible edits. Keep independently useful deliverables as separate outputs.

## Scope the assignment

For a focused correction, inspect enough context to protect continuity and make the smallest complete
change. Do not force a whole-video redesign onto a local caption, crop, cut, or graphic request.

For a complete edit, inspect the full transcript, visible coverage, and available assets before changing
the timeline. Form one concise editorial thesis: who the viewer is, what they should understand or feel,
why this speaker is worth hearing, and what progression earns the ending. Save a scene-level Director
Plan before broad timeline mutations.

Confirm intent before aggressive shortening, reordered claims, a hook assembled from later speech, or any
change that could make the speaker appear to say something materially different.

## Shape the spoken story

Build from complete ideas rather than isolated sentences. Find the strongest truthful progression among
orientation, tension, explanation, proof, implication, turn, payoff, and action; use only the movements
the material actually contains.

- Open with specificity, tension, consequence, curiosity, proof, or a clear benefit. Do not detach a
  dramatic phrase from the context that makes it true.
- Compress throat-clearing, repeated setup, false starts, redundant examples, and empty runway.
- Give mechanisms, proof, emotional turns, difficult ideas, humor, and reveals enough time to register.
- Alternate dense explanation with resets. Uniform speed makes every sentence feel equally important.
- End by delivering the opening promise through a conclusion, implication, resolution, memorable line,
  or supported next action. Do not append a generic call to action.

## Clean speech without flattening the person

Use transcript and silence detection as evidence, not authority. A pause may be friction or performance.

Remove an unambiguous false start, superseded take, disposable filler, technical interruption, dead air,
or redundant clause. Protect uncertainty, conversational texture, deliberate repetition, reaction time,
meaningful breaths, and pauses that carry emotion or timing.

After consequential cuts, inspect audio and picture continuity. Repair a visible jump with a meaningful
reframe, alternate source, cutaway, or scene change—not an arbitrary overlay. Check for clipped consonants,
doubled ambience, room-tone jumps, and unnatural breath joins.

## Plan semantic scenes

Create a new scene when the viewer's task, argument, evidence, emotion, time, place, or visual mode changes.
Do not cut scenes by a fixed duration. Give each scene a narrative role, viewer task, purpose, free-form
visual treatment, and honest asset strategy.

Useful scene families include clean speaker presence, speaker emphasis, identity, media evidence,
split explanation, structural explanation, data explanation, a designed full-screen turn, transition,
and payoff. Treat these as vocabulary, not a checklist. Several quiet speaker scenes can be right.

Make neighboring scenes contrast intentionally through speaker scale, composition, evidence type,
information density, motion, color emphasis, sound, or duration. Variety must follow meaning.

## Direct the visual system

Choose one coherent Frame or infer one restrained visual language for a complete pass. Keep typography,
palette roles, shapes, image treatment, motion character, captions, and density contrast consistent while
varying layout to fit the idea. A title, quote, comparison, diagram, metric, and evidence frame should not
all become the same card.

Add a visual only when it improves orientation, comprehension, belief, recall, feeling, or action.

- Let the speaker's face carry trust, conviction, vulnerability, humor, and emotional turns.
- Use lower thirds for identity, number treatments for supported scale, comparisons for real contrast,
  diagrams for relationships, sequences for actual steps, and annotations for evidence needing attention.
- Summarize the editorial idea instead of rendering a second transcript over the captions.
- Use crop and scale for emphasis, intimacy, reset, or transition. Protect eyes, mouth, gestures, products,
  captions, and destination safe areas.
- Classify B-roll as proof, example, orientation, analogy, atmosphere, or transition. Prefer no B-roll to
  misleading B-roll, and keep strong evidence visible long enough to inspect.
- Use generated visuals for concepts or clearly non-evidentiary illustration, never fabricated proof.

## Coordinate captions, sound, and transitions

Keep captions readable, stably placed, and subordinate to the scene's primary idea. Reflow them around the
speaker and evidence. Highlight only words whose emphasis helps meaning.

Keep dialogue intelligible and natural. Let music support the editorial and emotional arc, then yield to
speech. Change or remove it when the story changes. Use a cut by default; add a transition or sound effect
only when it communicates a real change or reinforces a deliberate style.

## Execute and review

Gather only the transcript, visual, timeline, and asset evidence needed for the current judgment. For a
complete edit, normally read the material, commit the Director Plan, shape speech, retrieve honest evidence,
execute scenes with the lightest fitting native or designed elements, and review consequential moments.
This is guidance, not a mandatory tool-call order.

Bind planned placements to their intended scenes. Keep source-clock transcript ranges distinct from edited
timeline time. Batch related changes when the receipt remains clear, and re-read state after structural edits.

Review the rendered result as a viewer:

- Does the opening make a truthful promise and does the ending pay it off?
- Can each cut be understood without changing the speaker's meaning?
- Does every visual have a job, with evidence visibly stronger than decoration?
- Are the face, speech, captions, and primary idea competing anywhere?
- Does the sequence have contrast and breathing room rather than mechanical variety?
- Are any graphics generic, repeated, unreadable, unsupported, or unnecessary?
- Does the result feel like one authored video rather than a set of independently generated cards?

Revise weak scenes directly. Do not hide structural or evidentiary problems by adding more decoration.
